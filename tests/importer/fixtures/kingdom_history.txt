Chapter 1

The Rise of Valdoria

The kingdom of Valdoria began as a loose union of six river villages along the Marrow River. In 412 the village elders chose a fisherman named Orrin Vale as their first war-leader, after raiders from the northern steppe burned the granary at Teshwick.

Orrin Vale was crowned King of Valdoria in 418 at the stone circle of Harrowgate. He divided the land into four wardens' districts and ordered every district to keep a beacon tower. The beacon chain could carry a warning from the northern border to the capital in a single night.

His son, Edric the Builder, took the throne in 447. Edric paved the Salt Road between Teshwick and the harbour of Port Calder, a distance of 140 miles, and founded the Guild of Weighers to keep honest scales in every market. Merchants praised the road, but farmers resented the tolls collected at its seven gates.

The Salt Road made Valdoria rich. By 470 the harbour of Port Calder handled more cargo than any other port on the western coast, and the royal treasury held, by the chancellor's count, nine thousand silver marks.

Chapter 2

The Long Winter and the Fall

In 503 the Marrow River froze from the first week of autumn until the spring thaw. The Long Winter killed a third of the kingdom's cattle, and the royal granaries, drained by years of feasting under King Aldous the Generous, could not feed the villages. Famine followed.

Queen Maren, who ruled from 509, opened the granaries of the monasteries and rationed bread by household size. Her Bread Edict of 510 is the oldest written law in Valdoria that survives in full. Historians credit the edict with ending the famine of the Long Winter.

The Red Fever reached Port Calder aboard a grain ship in 521. Trade along the Salt Road stopped for two years, and the Guild of Weighers disbanded in 523 when too few members remained to hold a quorum.

Valdoria was divided in 530 between Maren's two grandsons. The eastern half became the Duchy of Teshwick, and the western half kept the name Valdoria. The old beacon towers, no longer maintained, were used as quarries for farmhouse stone.
